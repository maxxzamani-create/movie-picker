What's actually built

One box. It's called
the BX-1.

Not a deck slide, not a mockup. A physical box that sits between a venue's cable or streaming box and the TV. It watches and listens to the picture right there on the device — no schedule from the network, nobody feeding it cues — and it only takes over during a real commercial break. The second play resumes, the screen is back on the game.

Where it sits — between the venue's box and the TV
How it decides — watches and listens, on-device
When it acts — only during a real ad break
If it fails — the break just plays normally
Kill switch — a bypass remote, no menu, no phone call
Its own report card — the box posts live counts publicly
IT STOPPED WATCHING AND STARTED DOING. Every number this page published before August was detection — the box decided and wrote it down while the TV never changed. That changed on July 30, when the watch-only safety flag came off. In a 79-minute supervised live-fire run, all seven commercial pods the owner called out loud were physically switched by the box: the relay moved, the screen changed, the game came back. Best pod was inside a second of the human on the way in and the way out, and one pod it caught with nobody prompting it. It now drives a real screen unattended.
HOW FAST IT FLIPS: on the frame-by-frame review, the venue's ad was up about 2 seconds into the break, and never later than 12 seconds. Coming back it's typically on the game about 5 seconds early, during the channel's bumper. It has never cut into live play.

The unedited single-take video of a real catch on live TV — filmed July 14, 2026 — is on the homepage, next to the box's live feed. A U.S. provisional patent was filed July 17, 2026, and a second provisional on September 3, 2026.

Why the break is the opening

The screens are the venue's.
The breaks aren't.

A venue pays for the TVs, the programming, the wall they hang on and the power they burn. Then the game cuts to a break and those screens go to work for somebody else. That's the whole opening, and it's not a small one — roughly a third of an average NFL broadcast is commercial time, and there's peer-reviewed work in the Journal of Marketing showing that promoting an item on in-venue screens raises the chance a customer buys that item, with the effect strongest for indulgent items, new items, weekends, later in the day, and crowded rooms.

There's an established way to sell into that gap: the free ad box. A company puts a device in the venue at no charge, detects the breaks, and sells most of that break time to national advertisers. It works, and it proves two things I'd otherwise have to prove myself — venues will accept a box that controls their break time, and putting the house's own promotions on those screens moves product.

But look at who pays. In that model the advertiser is the customer and the venue is the inventory. The room, the crowd, and the screens are the thing being sold. The venue gets a slice of its own airtime back as compensation.

BreakShark inverts the payer. The venue is the customer. No ad network sits on the venue's walls, so nobody upstream has to be protected and every slot in the break can point at the venue's own food, drinks and events. That's not a feature somebody copies in a sprint — it's a different customer. A business built on selling venue airtime to advertisers can't hand that airtime back without firing the people who actually pay it.

THE HONEST CAVEAT: being structurally different is an argument, not a result. It's worth exactly nothing until real venues run the box and post real numbers. That's the next rung, and it's the only one I'm asking anyone to judge me on.
