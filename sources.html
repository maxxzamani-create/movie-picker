Numbers we generated ourselves

Every first-party number on the front page comes from one method: a person watches a recorded broadcast and marks the exact second every commercial break starts and ends. That file is the key. The box's own decisions are then replayed against the key, second by second, over a window that must match the key exactly. Nobody outside the company has audited these yet; the files exist and can be shown.

How much of your airtime is already commercials

This is the part nobody disputes, and it's the whole reason the BX-1 exists. You are already paying to show this airtime.

63 minutes
Commercial time in an average NFL broadcast — about a third of it

An analysis of a full NFL season found the average broadcast carried roughly 63 minutes of commercials — 32.98% of the airtime — across 100-plus spots, with the average break stretching from 1:50 to 2:20. Over a 17-week season that's about 23.5 hours of ads per fan.

2:05 – 2:25
Guaranteed commercial break between every half inning of MLB

Major League Baseball's own pace-of-play rules mandate 2:05 for local broadcasts and 2:25 for national between innings, and 2:55 in the postseason. Nine innings of that is roughly 20–22 minutes of guaranteed break inventory in every single game — scheduled, predictable, and currently not yours.

Published, third-party

What a screen in a room actually does

+8.1%
Higher probability a customer buys the item that's on the screen

A peer-reviewed field experiment in the Journal of Marketing tracked 237 advertising campaigns and roughly 30 million shoppers over four years, matching who was actually exposed to a screen against what they actually bought at the register. Putting a specific item on an in-venue screen raised the probability of buying that item by 8.1%.

The conditions that strengthen the effect read like a description of a bar on game night: it was +13.9% stronger for indulgent items than practical ones, +20.6% stronger for new items, +4.3% stronger on weekends, stronger later in the day, and stronger when the room was more crowded.

Read this honestly: the study ran in grocery retail, not in bars, so we cite it as the best available measurement of the mechanism — screens sell the thing on the screen — not as a promise about your room. And it found the opposite of what most signage marketing claims: screens did not increase how much people spent overall. They changed which items people bought. That's a real limit, and it's also the honest pitch — this moves your mix toward what you want moving, it doesn't conjure new spending.
Peer-reviewed Herhausen, de Jong & Grewal · Journal of Marketing (AMA) Open-access copy → Publisher →
66%
Of out-of-home sports viewers watch in a restaurant or bar

Nielsen found that among U.S. adults who watched fall sports away from home, 66% watched in a restaurant or bar, and 31% of 18–24 year-olds watched while working out. Roughly half discussed an advertised brand with family or friends afterward. Your screens have a real, attentive audience in front of them.

24%
Of U.S. adults recalled seeing a video display in a restaurant or bar in the last 30 days

About 58 million adults. Venue screens are noticed at a scale most owners underestimate — which cuts both ways: they're noticing whatever is on there right now, too.

Age caveat: this is a 2013 Arbitron study — the most rigorous venue-recall measurement we could find, but it is over a decade old and the publisher has since been acquired. We use it for order of magnitude, nothing finer.

Published by a competitor

The one study that measured exactly this

Every other number on this page is either ours or from neutral research. This one is different: it was published by a company that competes with us, about their own box. We're putting it here, with the link, because it's the cleanest published measurement of the thing the BX-1 does — and because you should be able to check a number we're leaning on, especially that one.

+6.5% / +14.8% / +70%
Food and drink sales, featured-beer pours, and event turnout — a four-pub group, against a matched control group

A four-location Irish pub group ran its own promotions during the commercial breaks for one month, measured against a matched four-location control group that didn't. Against that control: food and drink sales +6.5%, pours of the single beer they featured +14.8% against other beers, and turnout at the one event they promoted +70%.

A matched control group over a real month is better methodology than almost anything else published in this category — including the vendor blog statistics that get repeated everywhere else, which is why we threw those out (see below).

The slot question, and why it matters more than the percentages. The case study says of the venue's setup: "[The box] is free, and we install it." The same company's published plan page says the free plan gives the venue "1 of 6 ad slots for your content" — "3 of 6" is their Premium tier and "All 6 ad slots" is Enterprise, both contact-for-pricing. (Brackets ours — we've taken the company's name out of the quote, not the meaning. The links below go straight to both pages.)

Put those two published statements together and the lift above came out of one ad slot in six. The other five slots were selling other companies' brands inside that pub, every break, for the whole month. Their own CEO has said on camera that the single house slot moves bar sales 3–5%; this pub group came in at 6.5%.

Where we're inferring, and saying so: the case study does not print the slot count. We are placing two of their published claims next to each other and drawing a conclusion. If that pub group was actually on a paid tier with more slots, the inference is wrong and the honest read is "some portion of six slots produced 6.5%." Either way it's four pubs, one month, on somebody else's hardware — not a promise about your room. We keep it here rather than on our headline for exactly that reason: our biggest claim should be ours, and this one isn't.

+287% / −28% / 60%
What the other five slots do — from the same company's advertiser case study

A spirits brand bought those slots across 200 venue screens for four months, roughly 50 million impressions. Published results: +287% increase in that brand's sales measured inside the venues, a 28% decrease in the competing brand's sales in those same rooms, and 60% of venues that didn't previously stock the product started carrying it.

We cite this for one reason. Every conversation about break advertising treats the venue's slot as the only one that does anything. This is the seller's own published proof that all six slots move product — and that five of them are moving it toward whoever bought the airtime. That last figure is the one worth sitting with: the ads changed what the bar itself decided to purchase.

Read it fairly: this is an advertiser success story, published to sell advertising, and a 287% lift is off whatever base that brand started at in those rooms — a small base makes a big percentage. We're not presenting it as a precise measurement of what happens in your bar. We're presenting it as the seller's own answer to "do the other five slots matter."

3% → 15.9%
Our six-slot model — what it is, exactly how it's calculated, and why we made it more conservative on August 2

On the homepage we model what six slots might do. This is a model, not a measurement, and here is the whole of it so you can check our arithmetic or reject it.

What we refuse to do: multiply. Six times the case-study result is 39%, and it's wrong. Advertising response is concave — each additional exposure does less work than the one before it. Any model that stacks linearly overstates the top end badly.

What we do instead: we take the competitor's own two published one-slot numbers — their CEO's on-camera claim that the single house slot moves bar sales 3–5% (we use the 3), and their best case study's measured 6.5% — and grow both on the same square-root diminishing-returns curve:

LOW  = 3.0 × √n   (their CEO's low claim, one slot)
HIGH = 6.5 × √n   (their best case study, one slot)

n = 6  →  7.3% – 15.9%   (2.4× each anchor — not 6×)

Why it changed on August 2, 2026. The earlier version of this model held the 6.5% anchor fixed and varied only the curve shape, which produced a 15.9%–18.2% band at six slots. That varied the wrong thing: the big uncertainty isn't which curve you pick, it's the anchor — a four-pub, one-month number with no error bars. The seller's own CEO quotes 3–5% for the same slot, and a model that ignores the seller's lower claim and builds on his best one isn't the site we're trying to run. So the band now spans both of their published numbers. The top fell from 18.2% to 15.9% and the bottom fell from 15.9% to 7.3%. Numbers on this page only move down when we change our minds — that's the deal.

The part most sites delete quietly

Numbers we took down, and why

These were on this site earlier, or were considered and rejected. If we ever pull a number down, it gets listed here with the reason rather than quietly disappearing.

$65,000 / yr
Our old homepage headline — RETIRED July 27, 2026

For a while the biggest number on this site was "$65,000 a year." It was arithmetic: take the 6.5% food-and-drink lift from the pub-group study above, apply it to a venue doing $1,000,000 a year, print the result in letters four inches tall.

Three things were wrong with that. It stretched a four-pub, one-month result onto a room we'd never seen. It assumed the lift lands on total revenue rather than on the items you actually promote. And it took a competitor's result and made it our loudest claim — a borrowed number doing our heaviest selling.

The study behind it is still up and we still cite it, in the section above, framed as what it honestly is. But the dollar figure is gone and it isn't coming back until it's a number one of our own venues put on the board.
