First-party — our boxes, our graded files

Numbers we generated ourselves

Every first-party number on the front page comes from one method: a person watches a recorded broadcast and marks the exact second every commercial break starts and ends. That file is the key. The box's own decisions are then replayed against the key, second by second, over a window that must match the key exactly. Nobody outside the company has audited these yet; the files exist and can be shown.

99.5% accurate
2,148 box-hours of live TV graded across twelve SBX-1 boxes (sport: 1,128 of them)

The record comes from our own fleet: twelve SBX-1 boxes on live TV, six on their own feeds and six twins that show the same picture as their partner for side-by-side tests. Each graded page has an answer key a person signs off; the box's decisions are scored against it second by second. "Accurate" is the share of live-sport seconds with the right thing on screen: the game while the game is on, your ad while the break is on. Both ways of being wrong count against it — the game covered by an ad, and an ad left on screen. For sport, over 1,128.2 box-hours: 99.5% of seconds right. Network promos and bumpers are not counted either way. Hours are box-hours: when a twin and its partner show the same game, both count.

What this is not: a guarantee, or a number from a bar. These are our own boxes on our own feeds. Sport is where the box is strongest; other kinds of programming grade differently and are not in this figure. The Founding 10 will produce the first numbers from a real room.

Published, third-party

How much of your airtime is already commercials

This is the part nobody disputes, and it's the whole reason the SBX-1 exists. You are already paying to show this airtime.

63 minutes
Commercial time in an average NFL broadcast — about a third of it

An analysis of a full NFL season found the average broadcast carried roughly 63 minutes of commercials — 32.98% of the airtime — across 100-plus spots, with the average break stretching from 1:50 to 2:20. Over a 17-week season that's about 23.5 hours of ads per fan.

Trade press CBS Sports, reporting a Streaming Observer analysis Read it →
2:05 – 2:25
Guaranteed commercial break between every half inning of MLB

Major League Baseball's own pace-of-play rules mandate 2:05 for local broadcasts and 2:25 for national between innings, and 2:55 in the postseason. Nine innings of that is roughly 20–22 minutes of guaranteed break inventory in every single game — scheduled, predictable, and currently not yours.

Published, third-party

What a screen in a room actually does

+8.1%
Higher probability a customer buys the item that's on the screen

A peer-reviewed field experiment in the Journal of Marketing tracked 237 advertising campaigns and roughly 30 million shoppers over four years, matching who was actually exposed to a screen against what they actually bought at the register. Putting a specific item on an in-venue screen raised the probability of buying that item by 8.1%.

The conditions that strengthen the effect read like a description of a bar on game night: it was +13.9% stronger for indulgent items than practical ones, +20.6% stronger for new items, +4.3% stronger on weekends, stronger later in the day, and stronger when the room was more crowded.

Read this honestly: the study ran in grocery retail, not in bars, so we cite it as the best available measurement of the mechanism — screens sell the thing on the screen — not as a promise about your room. And it found the opposite of what most signage marketing claims: screens did not increase how much people spent overall. They changed which items people bought. That's a real limit, and it's also the honest pitch — this moves your mix toward what you want moving, it doesn't conjure new spending.
Peer-reviewed Herhausen, de Jong & Grewal · Journal of Marketing (AMA) Open-access copy → Publisher →
66%
Of out-of-home sports viewers watch in a restaurant or bar

Nielsen found that among U.S. adults who watched fall sports away from home, 66% watched in a restaurant or bar, and 31% of 18–24 year-olds watched while working out. Roughly half discussed an advertised brand with family or friends afterward. Your screens have a real, attentive audience in front of them.

24%
Of U.S. adults recalled seeing a video display in a restaurant or bar in the last 30 days

About 58 million adults. Venue screens are noticed at a scale most owners underestimate — which cuts both ways: they're noticing whatever is on there right now, too.

Age caveat: this is a 2013 Arbitron study — the most rigorous venue-recall measurement we could find, but it is over a decade old and the publisher has since been acquired. We use it for order of magnitude, nothing finer.

Published research

The one study that measured exactly this

Every other number on this page is either ours or from neutral research. This one is a published case study, not our own measurement. We include it because it’s the cleanest published measurement of what promos in the breaks do, and because you should be able to check a number we lean on.

+6.5% / +14.8% / +70%
Food and drink sales, featured-beer pours, and event turnout — a four-pub group, against a matched control group

A four-location Irish pub group ran its own promotions during the commercial breaks for one month, measured against a matched four-location control group that didn't. Against that control: food and drink sales +6.5%, pours of the single beer they featured +14.8% against other beers, and turnout at the one event they promoted +70%.

A matched control group over a real month is better methodology than almost anything else published on break advertising — including the vendor blog statistics that get repeated everywhere else, which is why we threw those out (see below).

The slot count. The study doesn't print how many of the six slots in each break carried the pubs' own promos. Our reading is that it was most likely just one, which is why we think filling all six can do more. That part is our inference, not the study's statement.

Read it fairly: it's four pubs over one month, not a promise about your room. We keep it here rather than on our headline for exactly that reason: our biggest claim should be ours, and this one isn't.

3% → 11.9%
Our six-slot estimate on the homepage, and exactly how it’s worked out

This is an estimate, not a measurement. It starts from published figures for a single slot of promos in the breaks, from about 3% to the 6.5% in the study above, and grows on a diminishing-returns curve as you fill more of the break, because each extra promo does a little less work than the one before it. We never simply multiply: six times 6.5% would be 39%, and that would be wrong.

1 slot → 3.0% – 6.5%
3 slots → 4.5% – 8.7%
6 slots → 6.5% – 11.9%

Read it fairly: it assumes your room behaves like the pubs in the study and that you have something worth promoting in every slot. Screens can shift which items sell as well as how much. Plan on the low end; your own room will show you the real number.

The part most sites delete quietly

Numbers we took down, and why

These were on this site earlier, or were considered and rejected. If we ever pull a number down, it gets listed here with the reason rather than quietly disappearing.

$65,000 / yr
Our old homepage headline — RETIRED

For a while the biggest number on this site was "$65,000 a year." It was arithmetic: take the 6.5% food-and-drink lift from the pub-group study above, apply it to a venue doing $1,000,000 a year, print the result in letters four inches tall.

Three things were wrong with that. It stretched a four-pub, one-month result onto a room we'd never seen. It assumed the lift lands on total revenue rather than on the items you actually promote. And it took somebody else's case-study result and made it our loudest claim — a borrowed number doing our heaviest selling.

The study behind it is still up and we still cite it, in the section above, framed as what it honestly is. But the dollar figure is gone and it isn't coming back until it's a number one of our own venues put on the board.

15.9% – 18.2%
The six-slot model's old top band — REPLACED

Earlier, the six-slot model ran both curves off the single most favorable anchor available — the case study's 6.5% — and showed 15.9%–18.2% at six slots. Nothing about it was miscalculated; it was anchored on the flattering number and its band was narrower than the real uncertainty. The rebuilt model spanned two published figures (3% and 6.5%) and now shows 7.3%–15.9%. Listed here because a number changed, and this page is where changed numbers go.
