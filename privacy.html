Plain answers about an always-on device

It reads the cable.
Not the room.

The BX-1 watches and listens to your broadcast to tell a commercial break apart from a timeout. That sentence makes people picture a camera and a microphone sitting in their bar, their waiting room, or their salon. It isn't that, and the difference is physical, not a policy we promise to follow.

There is no camera on the BX-1, and no microphone. It has no way to see your room or hear the people in it, because it has no part that does that. What it watches is the video and audio already travelling down the HDMI cable from your cable or streaming box — the same picture and sound your TV is about to show. It is looking at the broadcast, and the broadcast only.
Side by side

What it can reach, and what it can't.

IT CAN SEE

The broadcast on the line

The frames and audio coming from your cable or streaming box, in the moment, on the device itself. That's what break detection reads.

IT CANNOT SEE

Your room

No camera exists on the unit. It cannot see your customers, your staff, your patients, your register, or your floor.

IT CANNOT HEAR

Anybody talking

No room microphone exists on the unit. Conversations in your building are not captured, not processed, and not reachable by this device at all.

IT DOES NOT TOUCH

Your network or your POS

No register integration, no card data, no customer wifi, no guest devices, no point-of-sale connection of any kind. It isn't wired to any of it.

What is kept

The broadcast is read and thrown away.

Detection happens on the box, frame by frame, in the moment. Frames are evaluated and discarded as the next ones arrive. The BX-1 is not a recorder, and it does not build a library of what played in your building.

  • Leaves the box A heartbeat — and that's the short list. Is the box on. How many breaks it caught. Which of your own promos is currently up. A timestamp so the counts mean something. That's what phones home, and it's what fills in your dashboard. None of it describes a person.
  • Never leaves the box The broadcast itself. No recording of the game, the show, or the audio is stored or uploaded. We don't retain, rebroadcast, or redistribute your feed — and we couldn't hand a copy of your Sunday to anybody, because one was never made.
  • Never collected at all Anything about the people in your building. No names, no faces, no voices, no payment information, no health information, no device identifiers, no foot-traffic counting, no demographic guessing. Your customers and patients are not the product here, and there is no data set about them for anyone to buy, subpoena, or lose.
  • Who can see it You, in your dashboard. Me, when I'm fixing something. That's the list today, and it's short because the company is one person. If that ever changes — a hire, a vendor, anything — venues running a box hear about it from me before it happens.
  • One thing worth flagging Our public demo rig is a deliberate exception. The test box on this site publishes a frame to a public link on purpose, so anyone can check that the thing is really running instead of taking our word for it. That is our own prototype in our own lab, not a venue install. A box in your building is set up for your building, and exactly what it does and doesn't send is confirmed with you in writing before it goes on a wall — not assumed from a demo page.
For the offices that have to ask

Waiting rooms, salons, and regulated floors.

Some of the rooms this box belongs in have rules about recording that a sports bar never thinks about. Here's the direct answer for each, without dressing it up.

  • Medical and dental waiting rooms No patient information is involved, because none can be. The device has no microphone that could pick up a conversation at your front desk and no camera that could see a person in your waiting room. It isn't connected to your practice management system or your network in any way that reaches records. There is no patient data for it to handle, and nothing about your patients is created, stored, or sent. If your compliance officer wants that in writing on letterhead for a file, text me and you'll have it — I'd rather write it down than have you take a web page's word for it.
  • Salons, gyms, lobbies, showrooms Same answer, same reason. Recording-consent questions come up when a device captures the people in a space. This one captures a cable signal and nothing else, so there is nothing about your members, clients, or walk-ins being recorded to consent to.
  • Casinos and regulated floors Ask for a phased look, and I'll agree to it before you finish the sentence. A regulated floor should not take a pre-launch vendor's word for anything. Start on a bank of screens that has nothing to do with wagering, keep the bypass remote on the floor, and put the reporting requirements in writing. I'd rather be on ten non-wagering monitors that your compliance people signed off on than anywhere near a book on a handshake.
  • The honest limit of this page This is a plain-English description, not a lawyer's document. A formal privacy policy and terms of service are being drafted with counsel, and they'll be linked right here when they exist rather than backdated or quietly appearing. Until then, what's on this page is the accurate technical description of what the device does, and I'll put any part of it in writing and sign it if that's what your file needs.
