Straight talk

Where it's perfect,
and where it isn't.

A commercial break and a 25-second timeout can look exactly the same on screen. The BX-1 watches and listens to the game itself to tell them apart. On streaming it hasn't missed one yet — including the first session where a human called the breaks out loud and the box was graded against him, and the run where it stopped watching and started switching a real screen on its own. The channel type that used to beat it doesn't anymore. Satellite still does, and that one we don't sell. All of it is below.

71 of 71
Streaming · caught

Every break, four different setups. An overnight NFL broadcast, a brand-new streaming app, a Roku session, and a supervised live test with a human calling the breaks — 71 breaks in a row, none missed, and not one false takeover. July 2026.

3
Short false alarms

All of them under 16 seconds. In the Roku session it twice mistook a product logo, and once a schedule screen, for a break. Nothing ever reached a TV — the box was in watch-only mode. We're counting them anyway.

16.7%
Of airtime called as ads

12.9 hours straight, nobody watching it. On live football, the box called 94 breaks — 16.7% of the airtime. Real broadcasts run 18–25% ads, so it's landing just inside the believable band. Six days earlier this same box was calling 44.5%, which is roughly double reality. That gap is what a month of tuning bought.

6 of 6
Spot-checked, frame by frame

We pulled the tape on nine moments from that night. Six were breaks the box called: every one was a real commercial — Olive Garden, a Toyota RAV4, a cancer-society PSA and three more. The other three were moments it deliberately stayed quiet: all three were live football, scorebug on screen. That tells you the calls it made were right. It does not tell you how many it missed — nobody hand-labeled that night, so we're not claiming a catch rate off it.

9 of 9
Hand-labeled · live · supervised

The first time a person graded it in real time. On July 27 a human watched the same feed and called each break out loud as it happened. The box matched him nine for nine, never once cut into live play, and typically landed within a few seconds of his call. About 1.9 hours scored — small on purpose, because we only count what we can verify frame by frame.

Under 4 min
Recovery from a dead plug

We pulled the power on purpose. A bar is not a lab — power drops, staff unplug things, somebody kicks a cord. On July 27 we cold-killed the box and timed it. In about three and a half minutes it had brought back every piece of itself — detection, audio, video, the evidence recorder, and the safety flag that keeps it in watch-only mode — with nobody touching it.

7 of 7
Live fire · it actually switched

It stopped watching and started doing. In a 79-minute supervised live-fire run, every one of the seven commercial pods the owner called out loud was physically switched by the box — the relay moved, the screen changed, the game came back. Best pod was inside a second of the human on both the way in and the way out, and one pod it caught with nobody prompting it at all. The safety flag has been off since July 30: it now drives a real screen unattended, and every exit lands on the broadcast's own countdown, about eight seconds wide including the relay itself.

~3 seconds
The hard channel · solved

The one it used to fail, it now catches almost instantly. On channels whose graphics bar stays on screen straight through the commercials, the box used to get in a median of 11 seconds late — worst case 97, which meant losing the front of a pod. So we stopped fighting the scorebug and built a detector that learns the bottom bar itself, then watches for it to vanish. Entries on that path went from about 85 seconds to about 3, and it re-learns the bar on its own each time the program changes. Same channel type, same hardware — different answer.

HOW FAST IT FLIPS: on the frame-by-frame test, your ad was up about 2 seconds into the break — sometimes before it even started — and never later than 12 seconds. Coming back, it's typically on the game about 5 seconds early, during the channel's bumper. It has never cut into live play.
TWO DIFFERENT TIMING NUMBERS, AND WHY. The 2-seconds-in / 5-seconds-early figures above come from frame-by-frame review of 23 breaks, measured against the actual first frame of the commercial. The July 27 supervised session reports about 7 seconds in and 5 seconds out — but that one is measured against a human watching the same feed and saying "break" out loud, and a person needs a beat to see it and say it. Two different yardsticks. We publish both rather than pick the flattering one.
A REAL MISS, THIS MONTH — AND WHAT WE DID ABOUT IT. Overnight on July 26th the streaming app crashed to its own home screen. The box sat there and called a silent, frozen menu as commercials for about four hours. Nothing reached a TV — it was in watch-only mode, like everything we test — but it was wrong, and it was wrong for four hours.

Here's the part that matters. The box records everything it does at five times a second, so the next morning that miss was findable. We traced it, found two specific defects behind it, fixed both, replayed the fix against the actual recording of the bad night to prove it behaved, and had it deployed by 11:39 that same morning. The box will no longer call an ad break on silent video, and the five-minute ceiling on any single break is now impossible to slip past.

We built it to miss rather than guess. A false switch puts your ads over live play — that's the failure that gets a box thrown out of a bar. A missed break costs one commercial. So the audio proposes and the video vetoes, deliberately biased toward missing. And the open problem we haven't solved: some apps splice their ads in on the server side, where the scorebug can survive into the commercial and fool the visual check. That one's still open, and it stays on this page until it isn't.
THE OTHER MISS — AND HOW FAST IT GOT FIXED. The same July 27 session that produced the 9 of 9 also produced a bad one. Switched to a college-football replay channel, the box caught every labeled break but was late getting in — a median of about 11 seconds, worst case 97 seconds, which means the front of one pod was effectively gone. It also fired three short false ad-calls during live play, 17 to 46 seconds each, and once hung 80 seconds past the return. We know exactly why: the box locks onto the scorebug to tell game from ad, and it doesn't re-lock when you change the channel — and gray night turf beats the field check on top of it.

That miss was found in a labeled session at 2:50 in the afternoon. It was root-caused, fixed, validated offline against that same day's recordings, and deployed by 7:42 that evening. Same day. And it is now closed: rather than keep patching the scorebug lock, we built a detector that teaches itself what the bottom bar looks like and watches for it to disappear — entries on that channel type went from about 85 seconds to about 3, and it re-learns the bar by itself whenever the program changes. That is the whole reason the box records everything it does: a miss is findable, and a fix is provable before it ships.

And nothing runs away. There is a hard five-minute ceiling on any single break call, regardless of what the detector believes, and since July 27 it can't be chained around by back-to-back calls. Every error in that hardest session stayed under two minutes.
ABOUT THE SWITCH ITSELF, HONESTLY. The catch numbers on this page — the 71 of 71 and the timing figures — are detection, measured in watch-only mode: the box decides and writes it down, and the TV never changes. That is deliberate, because it is the cleanest way to score whether it knows a break when it sees one. The switching logic was first rehearsed against July 27's hand-labeled session: all 22 verified breaks would have reached the TV, and a deliberately ugly stress window that threw 130 sub-second blips at it produced zero flips. Then it stopped being a rehearsal. Since July 30 the safety flag has been off and the box drives a real screen unattended — the 7-of-7 live-fire run above is that result, on real hardware, in a real room. Detection and switching are still scored separately on this page, on purpose, and both get reported here — pass or fail.
And if it ever fails? Nothing happens.

The break just plays like it always has — the game feed runs straight through the box. Lose power, unplug it, whatever: your TV is the TV you already own. And the bypass remote in your drawer shuts it off instantly — no menu, no calling us. Every catch is money you didn't have; a miss costs you nothing.

Straight answers

What owners actually ask

Does it ever interrupt the game?

No. Your broadcast is never changed, delayed, or interrupted. The BX-1 only changes what your screen shows during a commercial break that's already happening — and the game is back the moment play resumes. In the frame-by-frame test it actually came back about 5 seconds early, during the channel's bumper.

What happens when it misses one?

Nothing. The break plays like it always has — the game runs straight through the box. On streaming it's caught 71 out of 71 in testing. On satellite it hasn't cleared our own bar yet, so we don't sell it for satellite — and we say so right on the page. Worst case is the TV you already own.

I've got 14 TVs. Do I need 14 boxes?

No — screens are never what gets counted. One BX-1 sits on one cable or streaming box, and it covers every TV showing that feed. If eight TVs are all on the same cable box, one BX-1 covers all eight. What you count is simultaneous games: three different games on at once means three feeds, so three boxes. Two games, two boxes.

Don't take my count over the phone, though. Big rooms get this wrong in both directions — I've seen fourteen-screen bars that needed two boxes and six-screen rooms that needed four. That's exactly what a Catch Night walkthrough is for, and if the honest number is bigger than you hoped you'll hear it from me at the walkthrough. The whole rule, written out →
